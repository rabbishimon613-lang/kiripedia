**Tom Drake** is a former senior NSA intelligence officer and a former full colonel in the Air Force turned whistleblower.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:36:31" /> His first day at NSA was September 11, 2001; per [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou), Drake became aware almost immediately afterward of the agency's plan to begin warrantless wiretapping of American citizens.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:36:31" /> Drake is the source for the public account of NSA Director [Mike Hayden](/wiki/mike-hayden)'s September 11, 2001 statement to NSA personnel — that the agency had been *"waiting for a 9/11 so that they could implement the bigger program and grab everybody's electronics."*<Cite s="2026-05-04-cleared-hot-446-cost-of-truth" t="50:01" />

Kiriakou's framing of Drake's position: *"Tom Drake, the NSA whistleblower, will tell you that the systems already exist to spy on our enemies, protect Americans, and preserve civil liberties all at the same time. They existed."* Hayden's post-9/11 program scrapped that approach.<Cite s="2026-05-04-cleared-hot-446-cost-of-truth" t="50:01" /> Kiriakou gives the same account in more institutional terms: NSA had a competing software program that could weed out U.S. persons and target only suspect numbers, but Hayden treated the September 11 attacks as *"the opportunity that NSA had been waiting for... to just vacuum up everything and then sort it out later."*<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:37:03" />

## Stellar Wind

Per [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) — relaying [Tom Drake](/wiki/tom-drake) himself, *"a great great friend of mine"* — the pre-9/11 NSA program Drake had built was called **Stellar Wind**. *"Stellar Wind would be able to collect the same kind of data while protecting Americans, and [Hayden](/wiki/mike-hayden) decided no, we want to grab everything."* This is the program Edward Snowden later disclosed publicly.<Cite s="2024-11-13-julian-dorey-250" t="2:24:26" /><Cite s="2024-11-13-julian-dorey-250" t="2:26:36" />

Drake — together with NSA colleague Bill Binney — used NSA's internal whistleblower process to flag the unconstitutional expansion. Per Kiriakou: *"They arrested everybody. They arrested Bill Binney. Bill Binney, who lost both of his legs to a flesh-eating bacteria, is in the shower on a Saturday morning when the FBI breaks down the door."*<Cite s="2024-11-13-julian-dorey-250" t="2:28:10" />

## Escalation through channels, then HPSCI

Per Kiriakou, Drake escalated his concerns methodically before going public: first to NSA's Office of General Counsel, then to the NSA Inspector General, and then to the DOD Inspector General — who, rather than acting on the complaint, leaked back to NSA that there was *"a rogue officer"* in its ranks.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:37:34" /> Only after every internal channel failed did Drake go to the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence (HPSCI) with a fully cleared committee staff member, turning over documentation. He was subsequently charged with nine felonies, including seven counts of espionage.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:37:34" />

## Aftermath and personal losses

[Drake](/wiki/tom-drake)'s pursuit of internal channels ended in criminal charges rather than resolution. He was charged with nine felonies — seven counts of espionage and two counts of theft of government property, the *"property"* being defined as the classified information he carried in his memory when he left the building.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="6:16" /> He faced nearly one hundred years in prison.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="6:16" />

On the morning of Drake's arrest, the FBI raided his house and told his wife — also an NSA senior intelligence service officer — *"you're either with him or you're with us."* She chose the agency. Drake lost his pension, his five children, his wife, his house, and his career.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:38:37" /> The felony charges were dismissed only after he went bankrupt<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:38:37" />; he ultimately pleaded guilty to a single misdemeanor count of misusing a government computer — having looked at Facebook during a lunch break — and received eighteen months of unsupervised release.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:39:10" /> He subsequently worked as a technician at an Apple Store Genius Bar in Bethesda, Maryland.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="6:48" />

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) cited [Drake](/wiki/tom-drake)'s case as the definitive example that the internal whistleblower mechanisms established for national security employees *"just simply don't work."*<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="7:19" />

## Why the Espionage Act charge didn't hold, and why it was tried anyway

Kiriakou says the government attempted to charge Drake under the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act), but the underlying conduct *"didn't rise to the level"* of espionage. He dates the weaponization of the Espionage Act against leakers and whistleblowers to the tail end of the George W. Bush administration, a course continued under the Obama and Trump administrations.<Cite s="2022-08-27-kevin-gosztola-mar-a-lago-affidavit-unsealed" t="13:32" />

Despite that, Drake was charged with seven counts of espionage over a conversation with a *Baltimore Sun* reporter, even though the information he disclosed was clearly marked unclassified. The government's theory was that Drake, as a national security professional, should have known the information ought to have been classified regardless of its actual markings.<Cite s="2022-08-27-kevin-gosztola-mar-a-lago-affidavit-unsealed" t="20:50" />

## *"On 9/11 it was almost giddy"* — Kiriakou's account

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described Tom Drake's case as one whose story would make your *"hair stand up."* September 11th, 2001 was Drake's first day at the [NSA](/wiki/cia) in the Senior Intelligence Service. He described arriving to find an atmosphere he characterized as almost celebratory — NSA had developed programs they had been unable to implement because they knew them to be illegal. The attacks gave them the cover they needed: all they had to say was *"national security"* and they could implement anything.<Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="21:30" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="22:00" />

Drake identified a specific program called [Stellar Wind](/wiki/thin-thread) that could identify when a communication involved a suspected bad actor and intercept only those targeted communications — rather than intercepting the phone calls, text messages, and emails of all Americans. He said: *"Instead of just grabbing everybody's phone calls and text messages and emails, it only grabs the bad guys'."* He brought this to his supervisor, who told him: *"You're new. This is none of your business."*<Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="22:00" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="22:30" />

Drake followed every available channel: his supervisor dismissed him; the NSA Inspector General told him the mass interception wasn't happening (the IG wasn't cleared for the program); the NSA General Counsel told him he was *"in way over your head"*; the Pentagon Inspector General destroyed the evidence he brought. He finally went to the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence. The response: NSA raided his home and arrested him. He was charged with seven counts of espionage and two counts of theft of government property — the allegation being that he stole classified information *"by carrying it inside his head when he left the building."* All charges were ultimately dropped.<Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="22:30" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="23:00" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="23:30" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="24:00" /><Cite s="2022-05-23-danny-jones-cia-spy-betrayed" t="24:30" />

## 'NSA is intercepting everything'

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) says it has been known since [Tom Drake](/wiki/tom-drake) went public in 2002 that the NSA intercepts every phone call, text and email — the baseline he uses to argue that a program like the Pentagon's [LifeLog](/wiki/darpa-lifelog) could simply be picked up and monetized.<Cite s="2026-06-07-jack-neel-epstein-mossad-mi6-cia" t="51:10" /><Cite s="2026-06-07-jack-neel-epstein-mossad-mi6-cia" t="51:41" /> Kiriakou has also said Drake told him NSA has technology to intercept Signal messages before the sender even hits send — capturing the text as it is typed, prior to encryption.<Cite s="2025-05-05-austin-and-matt" t="38:48" />

## Friendship and outside consultations

Kiriakou describes Drake as part of a close circle of national-security whistleblowers — including Jeffrey Sterling, Bill Binney, and Kirk Wiebe — who remain personal friends and socialize together, an unusual dynamic among people in their position.<Cite s="2024-11-16-the-team-house" t="2:41:03" /> Kiriakou has also said he turned to Drake, as one of his more tech-savvy friends, to vet the technical legitimacy of an outside business platform, and that Drake judged it legitimate.<Cite s="2023-04-12-danny-jones" t="41:59" />

## *"Name one"* — the blood-on-your-hands tactic

Kiriakou tells one episode from Drake's case as the standard illustration of a prosecutorial technique used against national-security defendants generally. At a proffer meeting at the Justice Department — the meeting at which prosecutors set out their evidence and the sentence they intend to seek absent cooperation — Drake was facing what Kiriakou recalls as roughly fifty-five years across nine felonies, including seven Espionage Act counts and two counts of theft of government property. The theft counts covered information Drake had *"walked out of the building with… in his head,"* which Kiriakou's interviewer names a thought crime.<Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:17:58" /><Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:18:29" />

The prosecutors told Drake he had *"the blood of dozens of American soldiers on [his] hands."* His answer, which Kiriakou relays with evident approval, was to call it: *"Name one. One single soldier who died because I said NSA was spying on Americans. Name one and I'll take a guilty plea. Name him."* They backed off.<Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:19:01" />

Kiriakou treats the sequel as the proof. After the case collapsed and the charges were thrown out, the accusations remained posted on the NSA and Justice Department websites — but the slide accusing Drake of having soldiers' blood on his hands was quietly removed. His conclusion: *"They just make that shit up and throw it at you to scare you. They do it all the time."*<Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:19:33" /> He notes the same tactic is used on journalists at the pre-publication stage, where the warning that publication will kill a specified number of people arrives from the [CIA](/wiki/cia) — or, if the story is sensitive enough, from the national security adviser rather than the director.<Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:16:55" /><Cite s="2026-02-23-epstein-lies-satanic-elite-mossad" t="1:17:27" />

## On what actually endangered Americans

Kiriakou has argued that it was the CIA's torture program itself — not his own 2007 disclosures — that endangered Americans, citing reports from Human Rights Watch (2005), Amnesty International (2006), and the International Committee of the Red Cross (2007), all of which detailed the program before he spoke publicly, and arguing the program served as a recruiting tool for al-Qaeda.<Cite s="2024-10-06-joecat" t="30:00" />
