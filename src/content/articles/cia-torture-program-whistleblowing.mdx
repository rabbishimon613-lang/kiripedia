In December 2007, [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) became the first former [CIA](/wiki/cia) officer to publicly confirm the agency's [enhanced interrogation](/wiki/enhanced-interrogation) program, asserting that torture was official U.S. government policy personally approved by the president.<Cite s="2026-05-31-john-kiriakou-podcast-john-kiriakou-new-posdcast-12-secrets-that-have" t="21:43:00" />

## An incremental turn: the hooding incident

Kiriakou describes his path to whistleblowing as gradual rather than sudden — a slide into becoming *"the human rights guy"* that began years earlier in the field, the night a special-operations officer on loan to him brought in prisoners with hoods over their heads. Told hooding is a war crime under the Geneva Convention, the officer refused to remove them and threatened, *"I'm going to report you to headquarters."* Kiriakou shot back, *"I'm already reporting you for committing a war crime."* They reported each other. The episode crystallized, for Kiriakou, a broader pattern: *"we're just going to pretend these laws don't exist because we're the good guys."*<Cite s="2025-05-28-ironclad-hidden-terror-program" t="10:56" /><Cite s="2025-05-28-ironclad-hidden-terror-program" t="11:27" /><Cite s="2025-05-28-ironclad-hidden-terror-program" t="11:58" />

## Waiting for someone else to speak first

For years afterward, Kiriakou says he assumed someone else — a doctor present at a black site, an officer who had fainted during an interrogation — would be the one to go to the [press](/wiki/brian-ross) first. No one did.<Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="2:20:41" /> Of fourteen CIA officers approached about training in the interrogation techniques, he says he alone declined.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="04:08" /> He describes cables coming back from the secret sites in which staff from the Office of Medical Services objected on oath grounds — *"I took an oath to do no harm, I'm not doing this"* — while nurses fainted during torture sessions and some officers curtailed career-ending overseas assignments in protest; the doctor who revived [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah) so the interrogation could continue was nicknamed "Dr. Mengele" by CIA personnel.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:28:41" /><Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:29:12" /> He says a senior intelligence service woman was escorted out of the White House, had her badge taken, and was told never to return, on suspicion — never confirmed — that she had blown the whistle on the CIA's secret prisons; he says he never named her.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:29:43" /><Cite s="2026-04-29-jay-dyer-they-really-did-this" t="1:08:06" />

## Going to ABC News, December 2007

Kiriakou describes deciding to speak to ABC's [Brian Ross](/wiki/brian-ross) after concluding the White House would scapegoat him for the torture program regardless of what he did: *"In for a penny, in for a pound. Whatever he asks me, I'm just going to tell the truth."*<Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="2:22:16" /><Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="2:22:48" /> He says Ross first approached him in December 2007 claiming a source had said Kiriakou personally tortured [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah), which he denied, telling Ross he had been "the only person who was ever kind to Abu Zubaydah."<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="04:08" /> While he weighed Ross's invitation to appear on camera, President Bush told a press conference, looking into the camera, "we do not torture" — which Kiriakou, watching, called a "bald-faced liar" moment to his wife, herself a senior CIA officer — and then days later told a reporter that any torture was the work of a "rogue CIA officer." Kiriakou concluded the leak traced back to the White House and that he would be blamed regardless, prompting him to decide to go public.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="04:39" /> He blew the whistle in December 2007, telling ABC he was asserting three things: that the CIA tortured prisoners, that torture was official U.S. policy, and that it had been personally approved by the president.<Cite s="2026-04-29-jay-dyer-they-really-did-this" t="1:07:34" />

## 'I only wish I'd had the guts'

Kiriakou says the most important lesson of his whistleblowing was realizing he was not alone in his misgivings: the day after he went public, a retired deputy director of the CIA — a name the public would recognize — emailed him, *"You've chosen a difficult path, but I'm glad somebody did. I only wish I had had the guts to do it myself."*<Cite s="2024-07-27-saltcube-cia-enemy-of-the-state" t="1:01:27" /><Cite s="2024-07-27-saltcube-cia-enemy-of-the-state" t="1:02:00" /> He has separately described [FBI apologies](/wiki/fbi-apologies-to-kiriakou) — letters from agents who worked his prosecution, since his conviction — in the same register of belated regret.<Cite s="2025-05-28-ironclad-hidden-terror-program" t="39:23" />

## Congressional knowledge and the reversal under Brennan

Kiriakou says fewer than two dozen people in the entire U.S. government knew of the torture program when he was told of it, yet the congressional oversight committees both authorized and funded it — so later claims by members of Congress that they *"had no idea"* were, in his words, *"just a lie."*<Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="04:45" /><Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="06:18" />

Kiriakou says the FBI first invited him in under the pretext of an unrelated case similar to one involving a Japanese diplomat who had once tried to recruit him; the conversation turned amicable small talk into what he realized was an interrogation, and it became clear the agents cared only about his having disclosed the CIA's torture program to Brian Ross at ABC News.<Cite s="2026-04-27-dead-drop-s1e25-the-whistleblower" t="01:35" />

He recounts that the Bush administration investigated him for a year and closed the case, because it is illegal to classify a criminal act and the FBI had already deemed torture a crime. The Obama administration, under [John Brennan](/wiki/john-brennan), reopened the case, recast the torture program as not having been a crime, and charged Kiriakou under the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act).<Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="12:33" /><Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="16:46" /> He says the CIA filed a crimes report against him with the FBI within 24 hours of the ABC interview, and the FBI investigated him for a year, from December 2007 to December 2008, before issuing a declination letter finding he had committed no crime.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="05:10" /><Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="05:17" /> Four weeks after Obama's inauguration, Obama named [John Brennan](/wiki/john-brennan) Deputy National Security Advisor for counterterrorism, and Brennan asked the Justice Department to secretly reopen the case; Kiriakou says his phones were then tapped, his emails intercepted, and FBI teams followed him and his family — to church, to dinner, to Target — for three years before his January 2012 arrest on five felonies, including three counts of espionage.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="05:40" /><Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="06:50" /> Facing up to 45 years in prison, he took a plea to a lesser charge, receiving a 30-month sentence and ultimately serving 23 months; four of the five felonies were dropped because, he says, he had not committed espionage.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="10:07" /><Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="03:40" /><Cite s="2025-12-03-honesty-box-24-tough-questions" t="20:19" /> He says an attorney told him the prosecution was meant to frighten others in the intelligence community who might consider going public with evidence of waste, fraud, abuse, or illegality, and that Edward Snowden later told the *New York Times* that Kiriakou and NSA whistleblower Tom Drake had inspired him.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="08:59" /> Two of the FBI agents involved in his arrest later called his lead attorney to apologize, saying they had been ordered to pursue the case for political reasons; the day after his disclosure, a retired Deputy Director of Central Intelligence he respected had already emailed him, unprompted, to say the same thing from the other direction.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:30:45" /><Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:31:18" />

## Legal architecture and congressional oversight

Kiriakou describes the internal approval chain for the torture program as running from the CIA's Counterterrorism Center to the Office of Legal Counsel at the Justice Department, back to CIA General Counsel, then to the Attorney General, NSC General Counsel, the National Security Advisor, and finally the president — a process taking about six months with no court ever involved.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:56:23" /> He notes the Army Field Manual, which nominally governs interrogation standards, is only an executive-branch document that can be changed "with the stroke of a pen," meaning the underlying policy was never fully codified into permanent law.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="23:02" /> He argues the [Senate Select Committee on Intelligence](/wiki/senate-torture-report) and House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence were not real oversight bodies but "cheerleading committees" that emboldened the CIA's assassination, secret-prison, torture, and rendition programs during the war on terror, tracing the agency's lack of real oversight back to its first covert action — the 1949 Italian election — through the Church Committee hearings of the mid-1970s, which ended with Iran-Contra.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="13:56" />

## Aftermath: prison, honors, and later views on the CIA

Kiriakou says he would do it all over again, calling his only regret the failure to hire an attorney before blowing the whistle.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="03:40" /><Cite s="2025-12-03-honesty-box-24-tough-questions" t="20:19" /> He has since received the highest annual award given by the Irish government — a gold medal awarded yearly since 1777, whose recent recipients include the presidents of Ireland and Finland and five Nobel Peace Prize laureates — and a truth-telling medal from the government of Luxembourg.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="54:22" /> He has become a dual U.S.–Greek citizen who can vote in Greek elections.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="55:24" /> He rejects the label "traitor," citing the constitutional definition of treason as providing aid and comfort to the enemy in wartime.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="1:00:36" />

Kiriakou has said on the record that he believes the CIA should be shut down, noting the U.S. maintains 18 intelligence agencies — now overseen by Tulsi Gabbard — compared to the one or two most countries run.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="15:28" /> He calls himself a realist who accepts the CIA will never actually be dismantled, and advises anyone wanting to reform it to work from inside: run for Congress and join an oversight committee, or join the CIA itself and rise into a position of authority.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="54:07" /> He points to the Twitter Files' revelation that active-duty CIA, NSA, and FBI employees worked at Twitter, Meta, Instagram, and Facebook as reason for concern given the intelligence community's ties to In-Q-Tel, Palantir, and Abraxas.<Cite s="2026-06-10-zeteo-abolish-the-cia" t="39:13" />
