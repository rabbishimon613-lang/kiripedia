Kiriakou has said that he had a conversation once in prison that was so stunning to him he does not think he will ever forget it.<Cite s="2017-05-19-peter-b-collins-show-in-depth-interview-former-cia-officer-john-ki" t="32:26" />

## The scene

It was the middle of the day. All the televisions were on, but most of the men were at work or outside, so the unit was essentially empty. Three African American men were sitting in the television room playing cards.<Cite s="2017-05-19-peter-b-collins-show-in-depth-interview-former-cia-officer-john-ki" t="32:26" />

One of them looked around and took stock of the arrangement out loud. They got three square meals a day. They got a warm bed. They got clothes. They got television. They could play cards. They got medical care. And then the conclusion, which is the part Kiriakou repeats: if they had women, the place would be paradise.<Cite s="2017-05-19-peter-b-collins-show-in-depth-interview-former-cia-officer-john-ki" t="32:26" /><Cite s="2017-05-19-peter-b-collins-show-in-depth-interview-former-cia-officer-john-ki" t="32:56" />

## What he takes it to mean

Kiriakou does not offer the remark as a comment on how soft federal prison is; he has been consistently scathing about that idea elsewhere. He offers it in the middle of an argument about deprivation outside the walls.

Immediately before recounting it he had been describing what he says prison showed him about poverty: what happens in a community where everybody is poor, where people have no educational background or foundation in even basic education, and where they have no job skills — they end up in prison.<Cite s="2017-05-19-peter-b-collins-show-in-depth-interview-former-cia-officer-john-ki" t="31:56" /> The card game is the illustration attached to that claim. The men were not praising the institution. They were measuring it against what they had known, and finding that on food, shelter, clothing and access to a doctor it came out ahead.

That is the force of the anecdote in his telling: the comparison is only available to someone for whom those things were not already guaranteed. Kiriakou's own experience at Loretto — the segregation of the television rooms, the pedophile population, the correctional officers he had no time for — supplied no such comparison, and he has never suggested otherwise.
