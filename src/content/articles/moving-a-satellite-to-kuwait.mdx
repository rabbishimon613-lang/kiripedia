**Moving a satellite to Kuwait** is the anecdote [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) uses to convey how limited overhead reconnaissance was at the start of his career.

After Iraq [invaded Kuwait](/wiki/kuwait-invasion-intelligence) on 2 August 1990, the United States needed satellite coverage of the country and did not have it — because it had never needed it. Kiriakou says it took six months to move a satellite that had been sitting over Moscow so that it could show Kuwait.<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="43:16" />

He offers it in response to an account of the search for [Bill Buckley](/wiki/bill-buckley), the CIA's Beirut station chief kidnapped in 1984: a young analyst from the National Photographic Interpretation Center would take the day's single satellite image of Beirut and go over the rooftops with a magnifying glass, trying to match them against structures described in hostage debriefings.<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="42:41" /> Kiriakou's reply is that he can only imagine the work involved — and both men reach the same summary at the same moment: *"there was no Google Earth."*<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="43:16" />

The same absence shaped his own tradecraft in the field, where [planning a surveillance detection route](/wiki/planning-a-surveillance-detection-route) meant photocopying road-atlas pages and taping them together, and where the [hunt for Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/mr-fish) in Pakistan was conducted with 1961 maps.<Cite s="2025-12-29-dead-drop-s1e8-the-james-bond-academy" t="13:30" /><Cite s="2026-02-23-dead-drop-s1e16-captured" t="07:31" />
