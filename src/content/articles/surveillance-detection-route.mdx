A **surveillance detection route** (SDR) is a meticulously structured movement through a series of locations, employed by Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) case officers and their recruited assets. Its primary purpose is to ascertain whether they are being followed before, during, or after a clandestine operational meeting. The successful execution of an SDR compels any surveillant to either expose their presence through repeated appearances at improbable intersections or to abandon their pursuit entirely.

## The Fundamentals of Surveillance Detection Routes

The doctrinal pattern for an operational meeting in the field mandates a significant time commitment to surveillance detection. This typically involves approximately three hours of pre-meeting SDR, followed by two hours for the meeting itself, and a further two-hour SDR for the journey home. This totals an approximate seven-hour commitment for an asset to safely deliver intelligence to a case officer.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:23:16" /> However, in high-threat counterintelligence environments, such as those found in China, Russia, and Cuba, the standard SDR is extended to **eight hours**. This longer duration is deemed necessary due to the sophisticated capabilities of hostile services, which often deploy advanced technologies like infrared cameras capable of detecting body heat through walls and vehicles, as well as surveillance helicopters. In these environments, the cost of detection is severe, often resulting in the asset's execution. As John Kiriakou has noted, "They're going to catch the guy that you're trying to meet and they're going to execute him. So you have to really make sure that you're not being followed."<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="30:13" />

The mechanics of a properly executed SDR are technically specific and follow a defined sequence of maneuvers designed to expose surveillance. The process begins at a **kickoff point**, which is the asset's routine starting location, such as their home or office. From there, the route incorporates both "red roads" and "black roads." A **red road** is a major thoroughfare characterized by heavy traffic, where surveillance cannot be reliably detected. These roads serve primarily as connective tissue, allowing movement between different SDR stops. In contrast, a **black road** is a residential or minor street with low traffic, where the repeated appearance of the same vehicle or individual is more likely to expose surveillance.

Throughout the SDR, the asset makes three or more visits to **SDR stops**, which are plausibly routine errand destinations like a dry cleaner, a wine shop, or a fabric store. These stops are chosen to mimic ordinary daily activities. At each stop, the asset diligently notes the make, model, color, and license plate of every nearby vehicle, building a record to identify potential followers.<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="25:34" /><Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="26:05" /><Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="27:08" /> Following these stops, the asset enters the **provocative phase**, often referred to as the "stair-step." This involves a sequence of deliberate and unusual directional changes, such as turning right for a block, then left for a block, then right again, a pattern no ordinary driver would execute. This phase is designed to force surveillance to reveal itself by attempting to maintain pursuit.<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="27:38" /> The **final approach** to the meeting involves even more deliberately bizarre driving maneuvers, such as pulling halfway down a residential block, turning into a driveway, executing a U-turn, pulling into another driveway, and performing another U-turn. These actions are intended to expose any surveillance that might have remained undetected up to that point.<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="28:10" /> If surveillance is confirmed at any stage, the asset must **abort** the mission, returning home without making the meeting and re-attempting the SDR 24 hours later.<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="28:41" /> The operative definition of surveillance in this context is clear: "The definition of surveillance is multiple sightings at time and distance. So you see the car multiple times at different places."<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="27:08" />

## Counter-Surveillance and Hostile Adaptations

Hostile surveillance services are not static in their methods; they continuously adapt their techniques to defeat the layers of detection inherent in an SDR. Common counter-SDR tactics include **plate changes** mid-tail, where surveillance vehicles swap license plates to appear as different cars. Operators also employ **wardrobe and disguise changes**, utilizing wigs, mustaches, and hats to alter their appearance. When an asset transitions out of their vehicle, surveillance operators may execute **on-foot pickups**, abandoning their vehicles to continue pursuit on foot, via subway, or by bus. To counter vehicle-based detection, hostile services may use **pre-positioned rental cars** in parking garages, a tactic that Kiriakou himself employed in high-threat areas as a switch vehicle. As previously noted, in countries like China, Russia, and Cuba, advanced technologies such as **infrared cameras** and **helicopters** are integrated into surveillance operations, necessitating the extended eight-hour SDR.<Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="29:43" /><Cite s="2025-08-31-dalton-fischer-mossad-blackwater" t="30:13" />

A related personal-security discipline crucial for intelligence operatives is the deliberate disruption of one's daily **pattern of life**. A [CIA](/wiki/cia) officer operating in a high-threat environment must vary their time of departure from home and their route to work daily. Similarly, an asset under surveillance from a hostile service must consistently alter their visible routine—changing the time they leave home, the coffee shop they frequent, or the route they walk—to deny the hostile service a predictable opportunity for a strike.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:35:17" /> During [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou)'s tour in Athens from 1998 to 2000, a period when the [Revolutionary Organization 17 November](/wiki/revolutionary-organization-17-november) was highly active, his commute from his home in a northern suburb to the U.S. embassy could range from twenty minutes to two hours on any given day. This constant variation was a collective effort among embassy personnel, as Kiriakou recounted, "You're going to be in eventually. We're all working 12- and 14-hour days anyway, so it's not like you're not going to put in your eight hours. So we're just driving like crazy people all over the city."<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:35:17" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:35:49" /> During this high-threat assignment, Kiriakou was heavily armed, carrying a 9mm Glock in a fanny pack, a snub-nose .38 revolver on his ankle, and a buck knife in his back pocket, along with two extra magazines. A single lapse in this discipline could prove fatal. In March 2000, Kiriakou overslept, forcing him to take Kifissias Avenue, the most direct route to the embassy, which was bounded by concrete Jersey barriers, preventing any deviation. He arrived in heavy traffic to find himself alongside the vehicle of [Stephen Saunders](/wiki/stephen-saunders), a British defense attaché, who had been assassinated by 17 November in what was believed to be Kiriakou's intended place.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:34:46" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:35:49" />

At the commencement of any operational meeting between a case officer and an asset, a critical protocol known as **the mad minute** must be observed. This involves asking four standard questions in immediate succession within the first sixty seconds, ensuring that vital information is exchanged even if the meeting is abruptly interrupted. These questions are: "Were you followed?" "Are you in fear for your safety?" "How much time do you have for this meeting?" and, crucially, the case officer and asset must commit to a date, time, and place for their next meeting before any other business is discussed.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="2:11:30" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="2:12:00" /> This rapid exchange ensures continuity and safety in a high-risk environment.

## Operational Realities and Personal Experiences

Case officers bear the responsibility of verifying that their assets are indeed executing SDRs as claimed, recognizing that an asset's verbal assurance of having done so can be unreliable. The standard verification procedure involves the case officer requesting a surveillance team from headquarters to discreetly observe the asset along their asserted route. In one instance in Athens, an asset of Kiriakou's executed "a surveillance detection route that was so ridiculously good" that the CIA surveillance team was compelled to break off their observation rather than risk compromising their cover.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="2:11:00" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="2:11:30" /> This anecdote underscores the high level of skill and diligence required for effective SDRs.

Adherence to SDR doctrine is paramount, as demonstrated by an incident in Bahrain. A political officer, upon detecting surveillance during the second phase of her SDR, deviated from established protocol. Instead of aborting and returning home, she engaged the surveillance team in a high-speed chase across the island. The consequence of this departure from doctrine was her identification as an undercover [CIA](/wiki/cia) officer, leading to her being placed under constant surveillance every single day for the subsequent three years. In stark contrast, Kiriakou, who had been on the same operational day but adhered to the SDR by simply going to a grocery store and a hardware store, was never subsequently bothered.<Cite s="2026-04-20-tommy-g-israel-double-agents-directed-energy" t="30:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-20-tommy-g-israel-double-agents-directed-energy" t="31:00" /><Cite s="2026-04-20-tommy-g-israel-double-agents-directed-energy" t="31:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-20-tommy-g-israel-double-agents-directed-energy" t="32:00" /> This incident serves as a stark illustration of the critical importance of following established tradecraft.

Kiriakou's expertise in surveillance and surveillance detection was not limited to field operations; he also served as an instructor at the [CIA](/wiki/cia) in parallel with his day job, conducting training duties at night. He has emphasized the seriousness with which he approached surveillance during his Athens tour, detailing these experiences at length in his first book. His dedication to the subject led him to author a separate volume, *The CIA Insider's Guide to Surveillance and Surveillance Detection*, entirely devoted to the topic. He further shared his knowledge by teaching a university-level course on surveillance and surveillance detection at George Washington University.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:10:37" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:11:07" /> His commitment to tradecraft extended to his personal life, as he maintained the discipline of SDRs even after his departure from the agency. He quickly identified the FBI following him, noting that the population of plausible surveillants is broader than commonly assumed: "It could be a private eye because your spouse doesn't trust you, it could be a carjacker or a mugger, or the FBI, or the local cops."<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:11:38" /> Indeed, after a 2007 interview, Kiriakou was subjected to three years of intense FBI surveillance, including phone and email intercepts, carried out by three distinct FBI surveillance teams, ultimately leading to his 2012 charges. He only discontinued routine SDR practice around 2025, indicating a significant shift in his personal threat assessment.<Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:12:10" />

## The Enduring Importance of Tradecraft

The rigorous application of surveillance detection techniques is a cornerstone of clandestine operations, reflecting a broader commitment to meticulous tradecraft within the intelligence community. While some aspects of intelligence work might be perceived as "nonsense work" or unproductive, successful SDRs and other security protocols represent tangible victories in a high-stakes environment. The ability to effectively manage and protect assets, even in the face of extreme threats, underscores the value of these disciplines.

One particularly challenging operational scenario involved an asset who, unbeknownst to Kiriakou initially, had been given orders by a hostile service to kill him at their next meeting. Despite the grave danger, Kiriakou, having received intelligence about the threat, proceeded with the meeting. He employed a sophisticated approach to manage the asset and the potential threat. During the meeting, Kiriakou directed the asset through a series of movements within a hotel, instructing him to take the stairs while Kiriakou used the elevator to avoid dragging surveillance. Ultimately, Kiriakou led the asset to the hotel roof, a location where the asset felt uncomfortable and exposed. Through a combination of psychological pressure and maintaining control of the environment, Kiriakou successfully defused the immediate threat and extracted himself from the dangerous situation, demonstrating the critical interplay of tradecraft, psychological acumen, and personal courage in the field. This incident highlights that surveillance detection extends beyond mere vehicular maneuvers, encompassing the entire operational environment and the psychological management of human sources.
