**Guantanamo Bay** Naval Base, on the southeastern coast of Cuba, was a principal locus of the [Central Intelligence Agency](/wiki/cia)'s detention and interrogation operations during the post-September 11 [enhanced interrogation](/wiki/enhanced-interrogation) program. The base contains a secret CIA black-site annex known as [Strawberry Fields](/wiki/strawberry-fields), first publicly disclosed in the December 2014 [Senate Torture Report](/wiki/senate-torture-report).<Cite s="2015-02-09-democracy-now-torture-report" t="00:13" />

## Kiriakou's interim posting

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) served at Guantanamo Bay during the summer of 2002 as the [CIA](/wiki/cia) station's **interim Chief**, between permanent Chiefs of station. The assignment came shortly after the capture of [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah) in March 2002 in Pakistan, an operation Kiriakou led; he had become *"a big star after Abu Zubaydah"* and was approached by a senior agency official with the offer.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="13:40" />

Kiriakou had never previously been to Cuba and accepted on that basis. The summer was *"hot."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="13:40" />

## Inter-agency culture on the base

A distinctive feature of Guantanamo culture, in contrast with overseas U.S. embassies where agency personnel routinely greet and socialize with FBI, DEA, and ATF colleagues, is that personnel from different agencies avoid one another. *"You're walking past somebody, you put your head down — like, 'Who's he?' 'I don't know. And I'm not telling him who I am.'"*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="14:12" />

The [CIA](/wiki/cia)'s presence on the base is referred to even by other U.S. government personnel using the euphemism **OGA** — *"Other Government Agency"* — *"they don't even like to say the letters CIA, they just say OGA."* All other personnel on the base know which buildings are CIA and are told only that they are not to enter.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="14:43" />

CIA interrogations on the base — which for detainees including [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah) included [waterboarding](/wiki/waterboarding) — were audible to personnel of other agencies: *"You can hear people screaming over there."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="14:43" />

## Population and permanent limbo

Kiriakou says Guantanamo once held almost 800 people at its peak, nearly all of them — in his account — innocent of any crime, a population reduced by 2023 to 35, of whom only three to five were considered the "worst of the worst."<Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="25:00" /> Detainees cleared for release have often had nowhere to go: six Uyghur prisoners found innocent of any crime could not be repatriated to China for fear of persecution, so two were resettled to Switzerland, two to Albania, and two to what Kiriakou calls "Tahiti" (Palau).<Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="25:30" /> He says one detainee had been cleared for release for seven years and, as of 2023, was still being held.<Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="26:02" />

Kiriakou attributes part of this permanent limbo to a law, signed by George W. Bush and re-signed by Barack Obama, barring any Guantanamo prisoner from ever entering the United States for any reason. He cites the case of an Iraqi detainee with a deteriorating spinal condition who could not be flown to Miami for surgery once medical equipment shipped to Guantanamo for the operation was damaged in transit; the detainee, per Kiriakou, was left permanently paralyzed.<Cite s="2023-01-20-useful-idiots-gop-intel-oversight" t="30:11" />
