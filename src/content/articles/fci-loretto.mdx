The **Federal Correctional Institution at Loretto** (FCI Loretto) is a U.S. federal prison in Loretto, Pennsylvania. It is classified by the Bureau of Prisons as low-medium security — *"a low security prison, but it's called a low medium, and then there's a high medium. So this was low medium."* [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) served twenty-three months of his thirty-month Intelligence Identities Protection Act sentence at FCI Loretto between 2013 and 2015.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:01:33" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:02:03" />

## The countermanded sentence designation

At Kiriakou's sentencing, the presiding judge — over no objection from the Justice Department — designated him for a minimum-security work camp: *"no bars on the windows, no locks on the doors, you're free to come and go as you please, you're just on your honor not to abscond."* Most inmates in that designation work at a small nearby college.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="59:29" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:00:00" />

On Kiriakou's arrival three months later, however, the receiving officer told him his paperwork directed him not to the work camp across the street but to the main FCI prison. The discrepancy, Kiriakou subsequently learned, was the result of direct administrative pressure from [John Brennan](/wiki/john-brennan): *"Brennan was so angry at the shortness of my sentence that he told them, 'Make it as difficult as possible.'"*<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:00:31" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:01:03" />

A guard's only on-arrival advice: *"If anybody comes into your cell uninvited, that's an act of aggression."*<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:01:33" />

## The Italian-mafia placement

Within twenty minutes of being placed in his cell, two inmates — *"one with a swastika tattoo across his neck and onto his face, the other with FUCK YOU tattooed on his eyelids"* — entered uninvited and asked Kiriakou whether he was *"a fag,"* *"a rat,"* or *"a chomo"* (child molester). On answering no to each, Kiriakou was assigned to sit with the Aryans at meals.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:13:27" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:13:58" />

The assignment was overturned the following day by an inmate in the cell across the hall — the boss of the Bonanno crime family, known to Kiriakou as Pete — who, on observing Kiriakou's seating placement, told him: *"From today, you're with the Italians."* Kiriakou subsequently learned that another Philadelphia mafia inmate, Mark Lanzelotti, had — on reading in the *New York Times* that Kiriakou would be placed at FCI Loretto — gone to every Italian inmate in the institution to brief them: *"There's a CIA guy coming here. He's not an FBI agent. The FBI are cops and rats. The CIA protected us from the Muslims."* The Italian placement made Kiriakou's tenure at the prison non-violent.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:13:58" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:14:29" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:14:59" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:15:33" />

## The two documented intelligence-service entrapment attempts

Kiriakou identifies two episodes at FCI Loretto in which prison guards — per Kiriakou, on direction from outside the prison — attempted to provoke an incident that would have lengthened his sentence:

- **The Iraqi imam setup.** Three days after Kiriakou's arrival, a cellmate (an Australian arsonist) walked him around the facility and introduced him to an Iraqi Kurdish imam who spoke no English and had been jailed on a terrorism charge for translating a Stinger-missile bill of sale. Kiriakou was called to the lieutenant's office the next day and told the imam had — after meeting Kiriakou — called a number in Pakistan to arrange Kiriakou's killing. The imam was simultaneously told the same about Kiriakou. Kiriakou figured out the trick on noticing the imam spoke only Arabic and Kurdish, not the language of the alleged Pakistan contact, then reported the setup in his *Letter from Loretto* (published by Arianna Huffington at *Huffington Post*) — at which point Jake Tapper drove to the prison to interview him and the story reached CNN, *Time*, *Playboy*, and NPR.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:17:36" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:19:10" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:20:12" />
- **The Taliban-spokesman handshake.** A separate inmate (the spokesman for the Taliban, on transfer from New Jersey for four days only) approached Kiriakou in the yard with hand extended; Kiriakou observed a guard in the woods outside the perimeter operating a long-lens camera. Kiriakou refused contact, the spokesman left, and the inmate was transferred out shortly afterward.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:23:20" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:23:51" />

## The warden incident

After the *Letter from Loretto* attracted national press coverage, Kiriakou was summoned to the warden's office and threatened with solitary confinement. His reply:

> Warden, with all due respect, I've gone nose-to-nose with al-Qaeda, with Hezbollah, with the Iranians, and you want me to be afraid of you? Give me some credit. I've lived in Yemen, in Pakistan. I'm not afraid of your Loretto, Pennsylvania solitary. Besides — go ahead and send me to solitary. CNN's going to be waiting for you next to your car in the parking lot.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:21:15" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="1:21:46" />

Kiriakou was not sent to solitary at any point during his sentence.

## Camera resets and the midnight gap

Kiriakou disputes conspiracy theories about gaps in Epstein's prison surveillance footage, drawing on his own experience: *"Every camera that works resets itself every night at midnight. And they record over the previous day's recording."* He adds that cameras in federal prisons routinely malfunction regardless: *"The cameras never work in any prison. When I was in prison, because everybody has their heads up their asses working in these prisons."* The chapel at FCI Loretto served as a de facto meeting place for sexual activity among inmates specifically because *"the cameras didn't work"* there and everyone knew it.<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:03:43" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:04:13" />

## The Taliban spokesman setup

Approximately three months into his sentence, Kiriakou's attorney had warned him that the CIA was *"very upset"* about the shortness of his sentence and might attempt to set him up in prison. An Afghan-American pharmacist serving time for an opioid offense informed Kiriakou that *"the Taliban spokesman"* — identified as a man from New Jersey — had requested an introduction. Kiriakou declined. Days later, while exercising in the prison yard, the man approached him with an outstretched hand. Kiriakou put both hands up: *"Don't fucking touch me."* Looking past the man, he spotted a guard in the woods outside the fence photographing the exchange with a long-distance lens. Kiriakou refused all physical contact and continued declining until the man left. The man was transferred out after four days. Later, passing the guard: *"Nice try, amateur."*<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:20:13" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:20:43" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:21:15" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower" t="1:21:45" />

## Assassination setup attempt

Shortly after arriving, [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) was called to a lieutenant's office and shown a photograph of a fellow prisoner — a man he had met briefly days earlier. Officers told him the prisoner had made a call to Pakistan after their meeting and been instructed to kill Kiriakou. Skeptical of the story, Kiriakou later approached the prisoner directly and learned that the prisoner had been told the same thing in reverse: that Kiriakou had called a Washington number and been instructed to kill him. The two recognized the setup as a manufactured pretext and *"got a laugh about how ham-handed this was."*<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="19:50" /> Kiriakou identified the conduct as a Class D felony — conspiracy to promote violence in a federal facility — and wrote about the incident publicly.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="19:50" />

## Media access and the warden's calculation

After Kiriakou wrote about the assassination setup on his blog, the post was amplified by Arianna Huffington on the Huffington Post. This attracted visits from Jake Tapper, *The Economist*, *The Atlantic*, and CNN. Kiriakou leveraged his media access to write consistently about prison conditions, medical facilities, and guard-encouraged violence. He described the warden's decision not to send him to solitary confinement as a calculation: *"Oh, so I could have CNN standing in next to my car in the parking lot tomorrow? No, thank you."*<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="21:22" /> As long as Kiriakou did not name guards individually, the administration tolerated his reporting.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="21:52" />

## Mail reading

Prison officials were found to be slitting the sides of Kiriakou's outgoing letters and resealing them with tape. Policy in a low-security federal facility is to inspect incoming mail for contraband but not to read outgoing mail. Kiriakou went to the lieutenant's office and demonstrated the correct technique — rolling the letter inside the envelope with tongs to extract it without cutting — and told them they should use it. The monitoring stopped after that.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="21:52" />

## Guard culture

Kiriakou encountered hostility only from guards, never from fellow prisoners. He quoted sociologist Peter Moskos, a professor of criminal justice at John Jay College, whose book *In Defense of Flogging* described the Bureau of Prisons as *"little more than an employment agency for otherwise unemployable undereducated rural white men"* — people who washed out of the military or failed police academy training, paid modest wages but with federal benefits, and in Kiriakou's view inclined toward bullying because the position gave them authority over people who could not retaliate.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="25:29" />

## Prison social structure

Kiriakou described the social organization of the prison as resembling *"1950s America,"* with rigid racial segregation in all common areas including the cafeteria and television rooms. White prisoners were subdivided by allegiance: one Aryan Brotherhood table, one table shared between Italian organized crime members and Native Americans, and remaining white tables reserved for sex offenders and informants. Black prisoners occupied roughly half the cafeteria; Hispanic prisoners sat separately at the back.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="29:10" />

Upon arriving, Kiriakou was approached by two Aryan prisoners who vetted him with three questions: whether he was a rat, whether he was gay, and whether he was a *"chomo"* (child molester). Having answered no to all three, he was invited to sit with the Aryans.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="27:05" /> He instead ended up under the protection of Italian organized crime figures after one member — who had read in the *New York Times* that Kiriakou was arriving — personally explained to every Italian prisoner, including bosses of two crime families, the distinction between a CIA officer and an FBI agent: *"FBI agents are cops. CIA officers protected us from the terrorists."* From that point, Kiriakou sat with the Italians and was left alone by all other factions.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="28:07" />

## Violence

In two years at Loretto, Kiriakou witnessed three physical altercations: two over control of a television, and one in which an Aryan prisoner called a white inmate a rat and was beaten by him. Kiriakou attributed the low level of violence to the fact that most prisoners had worked their way down from higher-security facilities and were unwilling to jeopardize their placement.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="29:43" />

## Alliance structure on arrival

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described arriving at [FCI Loretto](/wiki/fci-loretto) with pre-arranged social protection from three distinct groups, and having to manage a fourth on his own.

The Italian connection came through Mark Lanzelotti, a fellow inmate who read in the *New York Times* that Kiriakou would be arriving on Thursday and spent the intervening days approaching every Italian in the prison — men named Gambino, Genovese, Lucchese, *"you get the idea"* — with a single message: *"There's a CIA guy coming here on Thursday. He's CIA, not FBI. FBI is rats and cops. The CIA protected us from the Muslims."* The Italians welcomed Kiriakou with open arms.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="10:19" />

The Aryan Brotherhood had received a rumor — origin unknown — that Kiriakou was a CIA assassin who had spent the post-9/11 period traveling the world killing Muslims. Kiriakou did not correct this: *"They thought that was fantastic."*<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="10:49" />

On the day Kiriakou arrived, the Nation of Islam newspaper carried a statement from [Louis Farrakhan](/wiki/louis-farrakhan) declaring Kiriakou a hero of the Muslim people for having stood up for Muslim human rights. The Muslim inmates had no problem with him.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="11:20" />

That left only the Mexican prisoners. In the six-man cell, four of his cellmates were from the Sureños, the Norteños, the Mexican Mafia, and the Jalisco cartel. One Sureño asked if Kiriakou was educated. When Kiriakou said yes, the man asked him to write his appeal. Kiriakou wrote it — it was denied, but he charged nothing. When the man asked what he owed, Kiriakou said he had people on the outside and needed nothing. The man put the word out among the Mexicans that Kiriakou was honest and had helped him.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="11:20" />

## The *"Cat in the Hat"* incident

A fellow inmate known as *"Cat in the Hat"* — named for an oddly elongated head — sought to move into Kiriakou's cell. Kiriakou's rules were no pedophiles and no rats. Cat in the Hat's stated crime was murder for hire; he claimed he had taken a life insurance policy on a business partner and hired a hitman to kill him to pay off a gambling debt. Kiriakou went to the law library and confirmed the story — but also found that Cat in the Hat had ratted out the hitman to reduce his own sentence. Kiriakou refused him the cell.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="14:27" />

Days later, Kiriakou was sitting in the day room watching a Steelers game next to *"Truck"* — a long-distance trucker serving forty years for strangling prostitutes. Cat in the Hat, standing two feet away without seeing Kiriakou, told another inmate that Kiriakou had been called to the lieutenant's office (Kiriakou had in fact been called to sign a CNN waiver for a Jake Tapper interview) and called him a rat. Truck overheard and alerted Kiriakou. Kiriakou — using what he described as [CIA](/wiki/cia) training — turned to Truck and said: *"Half an hour ago, I heard him call you a pedophile."* Truck rose without a word and beat Cat in the Hat so severely that a medical helicopter had to land in the yard to fly him to a Pittsburgh hospital. Truck received five additional years and a transfer to medium security.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="16:00" />

Six weeks later, Cat in the Hat returned to Loretto and sought Kiriakou out. Unable to make eye contact, he said: *"I'm sorry I called you a rat. I should never have said that. It wasn't true."* Kiriakou told him: *"As God is my witness, if I ever hear my name cross your lips ever again, you're dead and you'll never see it coming."*<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="16:32" />

## Taliban-spokesman setup attempt

Before Kiriakou began serving his sentence, his lead attorney pulled him aside and warned him directly: *"the CIA is furious that you got such a short sentence and they're going to try to set you up once you get there."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="05:49" /> About six weeks into his sentence, an Afghan-American pharmacist serving time on an opioid charge told Kiriakou that the spokesman for the Taliban — a New Jersey case — had just transferred to Loretto and wanted to meet him. Kiriakou declined. Days later, the man walked toward him on the outdoor track with his hand extended. Kiriakou put his hands up and refused all contact. Looking past the man, he spotted a guard standing outside the fence in the woods, photographing the encounter with a telephoto lens — *"a guard in the woods outside the fence with a camera with a telephoto lens going tick tick tick."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="06:20" /> The man said they had a lot in common. Kiriakou replied that the only thing they had in common was that he'd tried to kill people like him. The man was transferred out of the prison within five days.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="06:20" /> Kiriakou described the incident as the CIA's attempt to photograph him shaking hands with a Taliban figure.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="24:20" />

## FOIA request from inside

Kiriakou filed a Freedom of Information Act request on himself while at Loretto. The response was 250 pages, mostly administrative material — visitor logs, medical records. But four to six pages were marked FOIA-exempt with instructions not to release them to the inmate. Either through incompetence or deliberate sympathy, those pages came through anyway. The most significant document was a memo from the warden to all staff, written a week before Kiriakou arrived, with a notice in large block letters: **CAUTION: INMATE HAS ACCESS TO THE MEDIA.**<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="21:15" />

The stated reason Kiriakou had been placed in a medium-security facility rather than the minimum-security camp the sentencing judge ordered was that he was trained in escape techniques. Kiriakou noted there is no such training, called it a misreading of spy movies, and told the warden directly: *"If I had gone to the camp, I would have kept my head down, done my twenty-three months, and gone home quietly. But you had to make a big production out of it."* He then committed to accepting every interview request he received for the duration of his sentence — CNN, ABC News, *The Atlantic*, *The Economist*, *Playboy*, *Time*, NPR twice, and Jake Tapper at FCI Loretto with the warden sitting beside him. When Tapper asked what his biggest daily challenge was, Kiriakou pointed at the warden.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="21:47" />

## Warden confrontation over solitary

When the warden threatened Kiriakou with solitary confinement, Kiriakou got in his face and said: *"Warden, I have gone nose-to-nose with al-Qaeda, with Hezbollah, with the Iranians, and you want me to be afraid of you? Come on, give me some credit. And solitary? I've lived in far worse places than Loretto, Pennsylvania. Besides, I could stand to lose a couple of pounds."* The warden backed off.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="19:42" />

## Guard who wore a stab vest

One Italian inmate told Kiriakou about a guard so unpopular with the population that he was the only officer at Loretto who wore a stab vest — confident someone would eventually try to stab him. When the Italian mentioned to this guard that Kiriakou was in his housing unit, the guard said: *"I never mess with that guy. That's all I need — work eight hours and then go outside and CNN is standing next to my car. No thanks."*<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-escape-system-stop-being-manipulated" t="20:13" />

## Sentenced to camp, delivered to prison

At sentencing, [Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou)'s attorneys requested placement in a minimum-security work camp — a facility with no bars, unlocked doors, and a work arrangement with a nearby university. The prosecutor offered no objection; the judge agreed.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="40:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="40:30" />

On the day of self-surrender — in the American system, the defendant drives to the facility and knocks on the door — Kiriakou drove to the camp with his lawyers, his cousin and cousin's son, and a documentary film crew. He knocked on the camp door. The officer told him: *"You have to go across the street to the actual prison. They'll process you and bring you back."*<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="40:30" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="41:00" />

At the actual prison, after processing, the guard began walking him toward the back of the facility. Kiriakou said: *"Wait — I'm supposed to be at the camp across the street."* The guard replied: *"Not according to my paperwork."* Kiriakou told himself: *"Take it easy. They'll put you in solitary if you say anything."* He said nothing. He was placed in a four-man cell that, due to overcrowding, held six men.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="41:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="41:30" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="42:00" />

It took four to five days to get access to a phone to call his attorney. His lawyer told him that while a motion could be made, a court date would be two years away: *"Buddy, I'm sorry. You're going to have to tough it out."* Kiriakou served the full twenty-three months in the actual prison, not the camp.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="42:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="42:30" />

## Janitor, 16 cents an hour

Kiriakou worked as a janitor in the prison chapel, earning sixteen cents an hour. When he offered to teach a class — citing his PhD coursework in international affairs — he was told: *"If we wanted you to teach a class, we'd ask you to teach a class. Now pick up the broom."* He swept floors for sixteen cents an hour.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="1:04:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="1:04:30" />

## Cameras never work — the Epstein context

Kiriakou described the chapel as a frequent location for inmates to have sex because the security cameras there never functioned. He identified other camera-dead zones throughout the facility: the stairwell to the mail room, the hallway to the laundry. Inmates knew which cameras worked and which did not. Guards, in his description, were frequently asleep. He used this institutional reality to argue that [Jeffrey Epstein](/wiki/jeffrey-epstein)'s death in federal custody being attributed to camera failure and sleeping guards was not remarkable — it was simply how the Bureau of Prisons operates.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="1:03:30" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="1:04:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="1:04:30" />

## FOIA request from inside — the smoking memo (Bopst v2, April 2026)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) filed a Freedom of Information Act request on himself while incarcerated at [FCI Loretto](/wiki/fci-loretto). The response was 250 pages, mostly routine — visitor logs, medical records. But the packet included four to six pages clearly marked FOIA-exempt and not-for-inmate-release. He received them anyway, either through negligence at the Bureau of Prisons FOIA office or, in his speculation, because someone took pity on him.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="20:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="21:00" />

One document stood out: a memo from the warden to all staff, dated a week before his arrival, bearing the instruction in large block letters: **CAUTION: INMATE HAS ACCESS TO THE MEDIA.**<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="21:00" /><Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="21:30" />

A second item in the exempt pages cited his [CIA](/wiki/cia) training in *"escape techniques"* as the stated reason he had not been placed at the minimum-security camp as ordered by the judge. Kiriakou's assessment: there is no CIA training in escape techniques, and whoever wrote the memo watched too many movies. The real reason, he concluded, was in the first memo.<Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="21:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-23-doug-bopst-cia-whistleblower-escape-system" t="22:00" />

## Taliban-spokesman setup attempt (Morgan v2)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described what he characterized as an attempted setup operation against him while incarcerated at [FCI Loretto](/wiki/fci-loretto). An Afghan-American pharmacist at the facility introduced himself and, over the course of several conversations, began raising the topic of Taliban media work. He suggested Kiriakou should become a spokesman for the Taliban after his release.<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="44:00" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="44:30" />

One day, the pharmacist invited [Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) into the woods behind the facility. Kiriakou noticed a guard standing in the trees holding a camera. He kept his hands raised and visible throughout the conversation and declined every suggestion the pharmacist made. His private assessment: *"Nice try, amateur."*<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="44:30" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="45:00" />

Within four days, Kiriakou was transferred to a different facility. He stated that in his view, the sole purpose of the transfer was to stage and document the woods encounter, and that when the setup failed to produce usable footage or statements, the government moved him rather than maintain the operation.<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="45:00" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="45:30" />

## Crooked guard — pork loin, wine, thirty-five pounds

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described gaining thirty-five pounds during his incarceration at [FCI Loretto](/wiki/fci-loretto). The Italian inmates had a corrupt guard on their payroll who brought in pork loin, wine for Marsala sauce, and fresh vegetables. Kiriakou ate alongside the Italian families — names Gambino, Lucchese, Bonanno, Genovese — and describes the experience as eating like kings. His wife noticed the weight gain and asked: *"What's happening to you?"* He compared the atmosphere to a scene from *Goodfellas*, adding: *"It was real life."*<Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="22:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="23:00" /><Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="23:30" />

## *"Letter from Loretto"* and the Iraqi-Kurd setup

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described how his prison writing began. An attorney had set up a 600-person mailing list to circulate updates on his condition. After about six weeks, he wrote his first piece — which he called *"Letter from Loretto,"* consciously modeled on Martin Luther King's *"Letter from Birmingham Jail."*<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="11:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="12:00" />

The *"Letter from Loretto"* described a setup. Another prisoner introduced Kiriakou to an Iraqi prisoner; Kiriakou greeted him in Arabic, explained he'd worked for the [CIA](/wiki/cia) and loved Arab culture, shook hands, and walked away. Two days later, Kiriakou was called to the lieutenant's office and shown the prisoner's photo. The officers claimed that after the meeting, the Iraqi had made a call to Pakistan and was told to kill Kiriakou. Kiriakou's response: *"I could kill this guy with my thumb."*<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="12:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="13:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="13:30" />

The story didn't add up — the man was an Iraqi Kurd; why would he call a number in Pakistan where they speak neither Arabic nor Kurdish? Kiriakou approached him in the yard. The Kurd revealed he had been told the opposite: that after the meeting, Kiriakou had called a Washington number and been told to kill *him*. They laughed at how *"ham-handed"* the manipulation was. Kiriakou looked it up in the law library — it was a Class D felony, conspiracy to promote violence in a federal facility. The blog post went viral after Arianna Huffington picked it up; Jake Tapper, *The Economist*, *The Atlantic*, and CNN came to interview him.<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="13:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="14:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="14:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="15:00" />

## Why they couldn't cut him off from media

Kiriakou later learned why the prison wouldn't silence him: a guard had asked the warden why he didn't just send Kiriakou to solitary. The warden replied: *"So I could have CNN standing next to my car in the parking lot tomorrow? No, thank you."*<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="16:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="16:30" />

## Mail tampering — *"you morons"*

When correspondents reported their letters had been slit open and resealed, Kiriakou went to the lieutenants' office and offered *"intelligence best practices"*: rather than slitting the envelope, use a long tong to hook the letter, twist it into a thin roll, and pull it out the side without cutting. *"Just so you know, you morons."* They stopped opening his letters.<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="17:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="17:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="18:00" />

## *In Defense of Flogging*

Kiriakou cited Dr. Peter Moskos's book *In Defense of Flogging*, which argues the Bureau of Prisons functions as *"an employment agency for otherwise unemployable, undereducated rural white men."* He noted prisons are sited in rural areas because they're often the only local industry, attracting people who washed out of the military or police academies — people who, in his assessment, *"were probably bullied as kids and never got over it"* and use the job to bully prisoners who can't fight back. His only problems in prison came from guards, never prisoners.<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="19:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="19:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="20:00" />

## Racial segregation — *"1950s America"*

Kiriakou described prison as *"walking into 1950s America"* with hard racial segregation: whites and blacks completely separated in dining, TV, and rooming. One table was Aryans; one half Italians and half Native Americans; other white tables were for pedophiles and informants; half the cafeteria was Black; Hispanics sat in the back. In two years he saw only three fights — two over what to watch on television.<Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="22:30" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="23:00" /><Cite s="2025-09-09-dr-phil-podcast-part-2" t="23:30" />

## Private prisons and the [private prison system](/wiki/private-prison-system)

Kiriakou says roughly half of U.S. prisons are privately operated, citing the GEO Corporation, and that private operators profit by keeping every bed filled while cutting costs on food and medical care — feeding inmates *"animal grade food instead of human grade food"* and withholding medication.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="24:44" />

## Losing federal gun rights

As a convicted felon, Kiriakou lost his federal gun rights for life. After his release, Virginia Governor Terry McAuliffe — who had followed the case and believed Kiriakou had been wronged, though Kiriakou had committed no state crime and had not sought a pardon — granted him a symbolic state pardon restoring his Virginia gun rights. His federal gun rights remain revoked; a felon caught in possession of a firearm faces a mandatory minimum five-year federal sentence.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="27:19" />

## Overcrowding

Kiriakou says the building that houses FCI Loretto was not designed for the population it held: rooms built for two people were packed with four, and rooms built for four were packed with eight — *"if you were lucky,"* six.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="25:15" />

## Guard qualifications and competence

Kiriakou says the only qualifications required to become a federal prison guard are working toward a GED — no high-school diploma required — and no felony convictions.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="30:26" /> He says Bureau of Prisons policy assigns mail distribution to prisoners rather than guards for a specific reason: *"Because the guards can't read."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="30:26" />

He illustrates the hiring bar with an anecdote from FCI Loretto: when prisoners from a minimum-security camp across the street walked away toward the highway, the pizza delivery driver who happened to be dropping off an order noticed them and alerted the prison, helping guards catch the escapees. The prison hired him as a guard almost immediately — *"his only qualification was that he had two eyes and they both worked and he could read."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="31:29" />

Guard pay compounds the problem. While being transported in shackles to an outside orthopedist to treat a broken pinky finger, Kiriakou overheard one guard tell another he had been passed over for a promotion from GS-3 to GS-4 — a position paying roughly $20,000–22,000 a year at the time.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="33:09" /> Kiriakou has separately cited sociologist Peter Moskos's description of the Bureau of Prisons as *"little more than an employment agency for otherwise unemployable undereducated rural white men."*<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="25:29" />

## Guard corruption: the Pop-Tart pallet

Kiriakou told the Federal Bureau of Prisons director directly that guard theft of commissary shipments is routine — citing, as an example, an entire pallet of Pop-Tarts worth roughly $5,000. According to Kiriakou, a guard backs a personal Ford F-150 pickup truck up to the loading area, loads the entire pallet, and resells it on Facebook Marketplace or eBay.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="34:42" />

## The terminally ill inmate

Kiriakou describes a warden pressuring a terminally ill inmate to sign a waiver promising not to sue the institution, in exchange for release to die at home. The inmate refused. He died alone in a steel prison bunk.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="35:43" />

## The guard called "Blue"

A particularly cruel guard at FCI Loretto, known to inmates only by the nickname "Blue," concealed his real name tag with masking tape and was the only guard who wore a stab vest. When Blue learned from a friend of Kiriakou's that he would be assigned to Kiriakou's housing unit, he said he wanted no part of it: *"The CIA guy, I never mess with that guy... I go out to my car and CNN's standing next to it. No thanks."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="37:46" />

## FOIA request — the warden's memo

Kiriakou filed a Freedom of Information Act request on himself and received roughly 250 pages about six weeks later. Among them was a memo the warden had circulated to all staff before Kiriakou's arrival, bearing the instruction in block letters: **CAUTION: INMATE HAS ACCESS TO THE MEDIA.**<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="38:47" />

## Diesel therapy and Senator Mike Lee

Kiriakou describes [diesel therapy](/wiki/diesel-therapy) — the Bureau of Prisons practice of continually transferring a targeted prisoner between facilities by bus and plane, with no access to a phone, computer, or even pen and paper, so that no one, including family or attorneys, can determine the person's location.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="40:21" />

He cites Republican Senator Mike Lee of Utah as an example of how little leverage even federal lawmakers have with the Bureau of Prisons. A constituent's son in federal custody had suddenly stopped responding to calls and letters. Lee called the prison directly and identified himself as a sitting U.S. senator; the staffer who answered told him, *"You have a lot of nerve calling here,"* and hung up.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="40:21" />

## Advising the Bureau of Prisons on reform

In mid-2026, the director of the Federal Bureau of Prisons called Kiriakou and asked him to serve on an informal advisory council on reforming the agency. Kiriakou agreed, but told the director bluntly that the BOP would not succeed: *"You guys are corrupt from the very top to the very bottom and everybody in between."* His first recommendation, offered as a self-described lifelong union member: break the prison guards' union, which he says exists to *"support corruption."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="28:24" />

## Overcriminalization: two illustrative cases

Kiriakou uses two cases outside his own to illustrate what he sees as reflexive federal overcriminalization. A NOAA employee in Honolulu who ran weekend whale-watching boat tours was charged with a felony count of interfering with the feeding of a wild animal under the Endangered Species Act, after a tourist — not she — whistled at a pod of feeding orcas. She fought the case for six years before pleading to a reduced charge, and in the process lost her boat, her job, her pension, and her savings.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="18:26" />

In the second case, a colleague of Kiriakou's from his time as an adjunct professor at Liberty University had police visit his home after a neighbor's burglary. The officer, noticing his wife's prescription pills stored in a labeled weekly pill organizer rather than their original bottles, arrested her — a felony, since it is illegal to keep controlled substances outside their issuing containers. The couple fought the case for a year, ultimately pleading down to a misdemeanor with a fine and one year of probation.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="21:36" />

## Legal aid to fellow inmates

Kiriakou did legal work for other prisoners during his sentence. A Mexican drug cartel member in his cell — captured, by his own account, bleeding out in a gunfight with the DEA at a cocaine warehouse — asked Kiriakou to write his appeal. Kiriakou did so pro bono; the appeal was based on legal technicalities the appellate court acknowledged but ruled insufficient to overturn the conviction, and they lost.<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="48:41" />

He had more success with a fellow inmate he calls "Mark," writing a Second Chance Act appeal arguing that Mark's original sentence was harsher than current law would impose. Kiriakou was released in February; that August, Mark's mother called him in tears to say her son had been granted early release. Mark went on to become, in Kiriakou's words, *"literally the most successful real estate agent on the New Jersey shore."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="50:14" />
