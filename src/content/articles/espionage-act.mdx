The **Espionage Act** was written in 1917, a period [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described as *"one of the darkest periods in American history"* for civil liberties. The law was originally designed to target German saboteurs during the First World War and was championed by President Woodrow Wilson.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:04:40" />

Kiriakou has noted two fundamental features of the law that make it particularly dangerous as an instrument against whistleblowers. First, the act has never been meaningfully updated in more than a century. Second, it does not mention the words *"classified information"* anywhere in its text — because the classification system did not exist when the law was written.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:05:11" />

The act has nonetheless been used to prosecute people for disclosing information the government retrospectively deems classified. In Kiriakou's case, he was charged under the act for confirming a former colleague's surname to a journalist — a confirmation that was never published.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:00:27" />

Kiriakou cited the Obama administration's use of the act as a historic departure: between 1917 and Obama's election, three Americans were charged under the Espionage Act for speaking to the press. Under Barack Obama, eight people were charged — nearly three times the total of all previous presidents combined — and none of them was accused of lying: *"Not a single one. Because lying is not a crime. That's right. Telling the truth is a crime."*<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:05:11" />

The act's lack of a harm requirement was illustrated in Kiriakou's case at the Eastern District of Virginia, where Judge [Leonie Brinkema](/wiki/leonie-brinkema) ruled that espionage under the act required only that national-defense information had been provided to a person not entitled to receive it — no proof of harm to national security was needed, and no foreign recipient was alleged.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:35:48" />

Daniel Ellsberg contacted Kiriakou and asked him to go to trial specifically so the constitutionality of the act could be challenged. Kiriakou declined, citing his five children. Ellsberg then asked [Jeffrey Sterling](/wiki/jeffrey-sterling) to do so; Sterling went to trial, was convicted, and was sentenced to what Judge Brinkema described as *"Kiriakou plus 12 months."*<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:37:22" />

## Venue shopping, charge stacking, and no defense (News Beat)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) explains how the government wields the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act): *"venue shopping"* to indict in a court where conviction is certain — for him, the Eastern District of Virginia, *"where no national security defendant has ever won"* — and *"charge stacking"* to force bankruptcy.<Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="04:05" /><Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="04:37" /> Written in 1917 to fight German sabotage and never meaningfully updated, the Act does not even mention classified information — only undefined *"national defense information,"* so the secret is *"whatever the Justice Department says it is."*<Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="05:07" /> It allows no public-interest defense and has been used against figures from Eugene Debs to a Hollywood producer jailed for an insufficiently pro-British film.<Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="12:26" />

## Written in 1917, and unconstitutionally vague (Scott Horton, 2021)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) says the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act) is *"unconstitutionally broad and unconstitutionally vague"* — a view he says his own lawyers shared. It was *"written in 1917 to combat German saboteurs"* and *"doesn't even mention the words classified information,"* because the classification system had not yet been invented; it refers only to *"national defense information"* without defining what that is.<Cite s="2021-06-01-scott-horton-daniel-hale-persecution" t="37:01" /> He recounts a 2013–14 conversation with [Daniel Ellsberg](/wiki/daniel-ellsberg) about appealing an Espionage Act conviction to the Supreme Court to have it struck down, noting only two people then had standing to do so — [Chelsea Manning](/wiki/chelsea-manning) and [Jeffrey Sterling](/wiki/jeffrey-sterling) — and both declined to carry it through.<Cite s="2021-06-01-scott-horton-daniel-hale-persecution" t="36:31" /><Cite s="2021-06-01-scott-horton-daniel-hale-persecution" t="37:32" />
