**Congressional intelligence oversight** — the work of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence and the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence — is something [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) argues does not meaningfully happen. He raises it as the answer to an obvious question: if abuses inside the [CIA](/wiki/cia) are classified, and disclosing them is prosecutable, then who is supposed to be checking?<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="13:36" /><Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="14:07" />

## "A beef that I have"

Kiriakou introduces the subject as a personal grievance rather than an abstraction — *"That's a beef that I have. I feel very strongly about that."*<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="14:07" />

His comparison is with committees he regards as functional. Real oversight, in his account, happens with the Armed Services Committee, and to a slightly lesser extent with the Foreign Affairs and Foreign Relations Committees — bodies he knows from the inside, having served as a senior investigator on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee. Set against those, his assessment of the intelligence committees is dismissive: *"but the intelligence committees, forget it. They're just cheerleaders."*<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="14:38" />

## The chairman who wanted a proxy

The anecdote Kiriakou uses to demonstrate the problem is recent. A sitting senator — a committee chairman — called him a few weeks earlier and asked him to come up for a private meeting. Kiriakou agreed without hesitation: *"of course. I'm a patriot. I want to serve my country."*<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="15:09" />

What the meeting turned out to be about was a fight between two committee chairmen. Kiriakou's reaction was to point out how little standing he had in it: *"Well, what the heck do you want me to do about it? I mean, seriously, who am I? I'm nobody."*<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="15:39" />

The senator's request was that Kiriakou write something publicly — for a newspaper — making the argument the chairman would not make himself.<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="15:39" />

Asked directly whether he thought the senator was afraid, Kiriakou does not hedge: *"Yes, I do... I do."*<Cite s="2026-07-21-tucker-carlson-network-ex-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-on-the" t="16:10" />

## 'It took all of my energy just to not lose my security clearance'

The earlier instance Kiriakou gives of the same fear is a conversation from just after his release from prison. Invited to a dinner at the Greek ambassador's residence, he found himself face to face with a member of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, who came over to welcome him home and congratulate him.<Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="33:00" />

Kiriakou did not take the greeting at face value: *"Senator, I have to be honest with you. I expected more from you. I expected some support."* The senator became angry, and his answer named the thing he had been protecting: *"Look, it took all of my energy just to not lose my security clearance."*<Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="33:31" />

Kiriakou's reply inverted the job title. *"Oh, you're afraid of them. I thought you were the overseer."* His conclusion is the standing one: *"They're all like that now. They're just cheerleaders."*<Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="33:31" />

## What oversight would look like

Reviewing a scene in *Tom Clancy's Jack Ryan* in which a congressional committee openly challenges a CIA deputy director, Kiriakou called it *"very powerful"* and, in a perfect world, true — while saying it does not happen. His account of the consequence is a ratchet: the CIA *"has a way of pushing the envelope and just waiting for government to push back. And that's not a reliable strategy, because it will keep pushing and pushing and pushing until we have a torture program and a secret prison program and an illegal renditions program and weaponization where they take down their domestic enemies."*<Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="25:32" /><Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="26:03" />

The reason he gives is the absence of members willing to stand up, in either party — *"and I regret that I have to even say this"* — so that a hearing of the kind the scene depicts *"doesn't happen in real life. It should. It should happen every day. But it doesn't."*<Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="26:03" /><Cite s="2026-05-19-gq-ex-cia-officer-john-kiriakou-breaks-down-spy" t="26:35" />

## Cheerleaders, and the compartment problem

Kiriakou''s objection to the committees is twofold, and the second half undercuts any reform of the first. On their disposition: the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence and the [Senate Select Committee on Intelligence](/wiki/senate-intelligence-committee) *"are supposed to be the two oversight committees, and they''re really not oversight committees so much as they are cheerleaders for the CIA."* The consequence he draws is epistemic — assurances such as the claim that [MK-Ultra](/wiki/mk-ultra) ended in 1975 cannot be checked: *"How do we know that? We don''t know it. We''re just supposed to take their word for it."*<Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="08:26" />

On structure, he argues that even a committee inclined to look would be locked out: any surviving program *"would be so tightly controlled, so compartmentalised, that only the gang of four or the gang of eight would have access to the information anyway. And it would be so highly classified that they wouldn''t be able to say anything."* Asked whether the president would know, he answered simply: *"No, I don''t."* The mechanism of the deference he illustrates with [an encounter at a diplomatic dinner](/wiki/greek-ambassador-dinner-encounter) after his release from prison, in which a senator on the committee told him that speaking up for him would have cost his clearance.<Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="08:58" /><Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="11:34" />
