**Edward Snowden** is a former NSA contractor who in 2013 disclosed the scope of U.S. mass surveillance — including the [Section 702](/wiki/section-702) collection program — and subsequently fled the United States. [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) discusses Snowden as a fellow national-security whistleblower targeted under the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act). Kiriakou says Snowden himself has publicly credited two people as his inspiration: *"Ed Snowden told the New York Times that Tom Drake from NSA and I inspired him to do what he did."*<Cite s="2026-07-01-legal-owl-whats-wrong-with-prison" t="09:29" />

## Never coming back

Kiriakou says Snowden will never get a fair trial in the Eastern District of Virginia and is unlikely ever to return to the United States, despite having repeatedly offered to: *"that dude is never coming back to the United States... he has offered to repeatedly, repeatedly. The problem is he wouldn't get a fair trial — no way, not a chance — in the Eastern District of Virginia."*<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:41:46" />

Kiriakou frames Snowden's affirmative legal defense around a specific principle he says is codified in U.S. code: a program cannot be classified for the purpose of concealing a crime from the American people, even though there are other legitimate reasons a given act might be classified. *"It is illegal to classify a crime... you cannot classify a program for the purpose of keeping it from the American people if it is a criminal act."*<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="1:42:16" />

## *"Two awful slippery slopes"*

[Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) framed Snowden's situation as a genuine dilemma: on one hand, *"he breaks his oath and breaks the chain of command to leak information"* that carries real risk and sets a precedent the next leaker can point to; on the other hand, *"he had tried to go through the chain of command because he caught something that was anti-constitutional — it was cut and dry against the Constitution — and no one was doing anything about it."*<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="2:21:49" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="2:22:21" />

Kiriakou noted that Snowden, like himself, could not realistically use his chain of command: Kiriakou's own chain of command was [Jose Rodriguez](/wiki/jose-rodriguez).<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="2:23:54" />

## The torture-treaty distinction

Asked whether he would feel the same about his own torture disclosure if [Dick Cheney](/wiki/dick-cheney)'s office had technically changed the law to permit it, Kiriakou said yes — but called it an easier question for him than for Snowden, because the United States is a signatory to (and a principal author of) the United Nations Convention Against Torture. An approved international treaty *"carries with it the force of law and it supersedes any domestic law,"* so to legalize the torture program the U.S. would have had to withdraw from the convention entirely.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="2:23:21" />

## The Brinkema docket

Judge [Leonie Brinkema](/wiki/leonie-brinkema) of the Eastern District of Virginia — who reserved major national-security cases for herself rather than letting them be randomly assigned — had the Snowden case on her docket alongside [Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou), [Jeffrey Sterling](/wiki/jeffrey-sterling), Zacarias Moussaoui, and the Julian Assange extradition. The Snowden case never came to her court; Snowden left the country before trial.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="1:35:48" />

## Section 702

Kiriakou credits Snowden's 2013 disclosures with bringing [Section 702](/wiki/section-702) — and the broader architecture of warrantless surveillance — to public attention for the first time. He has cited the hundreds of thousands of improper FBI queries against the 702 database as the kind of abuse Snowden exposed.<Cite s="2026-05-04-cleared-hot-446-cost-of-truth" t="40:32" />

Kiriakou has separately criticized the *New York Times* for what he sees as insufficient coverage of Snowden's case,<Cite s="2025-09-30-deprogram-show-with-ted-rall-and-jamarl-thomas-deprogram-with-ted-ral" t="1:04:11" /> and has cited Snowden's own public call to change the incentive structures surrounding whistleblowing.<Cite s="2026-03-20-disruption-network-lab-war-military-exposing-crimes-is-not-a-crime-jo" t="1:36:56" /> Der Spiegel was among the outlets that published Snowden documents beginning in 2012.<Cite s="2026-03-20-disruption-network-lab-war-military-exposing-crimes-is-not-a-crime-jo" t="04:16:00" />

## 'They always move on'

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) says his then-wife urged him to keep talking about his own case, predicting the government would eventually shift to its next target — which it did, to [Edward Snowden](/wiki/edward-snowden). *"They move on. They always move on,"* he recalls, *"and she was right."*<Cite s="2026-06-15-jason-jones-3-hours-kiriakou" t="1:30:16" />

## Willing to come home (News Beat)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) says [Edward Snowden](/wiki/edward-snowden) told him from the beginning he was willing to come home and serve 10 or 20 years if he could mount a [public-interest defense](/wiki/public-interest-defense) — explaining to a jury that he acted for the American people. Snowden hired Kiriakou's attorneys, who raised it with the Justice Department; the answer was *"absolutely not,"* so they never took custody of *"the most dangerous man in America."*<Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="10:23" /><Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="10:54" /><Cite s="2024-07-29-news-beat-assange-israel-security-state" t="11:25" />
