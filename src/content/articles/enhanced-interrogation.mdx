**Enhanced interrogation techniques** (EIT) was the official designation used by the United States [Central Intelligence Agency](/wiki/cia) for a set of interrogation methods authorized for use on detainees in the years following the September 11, 2001 attacks. [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) describes the program consistently as torture and the official terminology as a euphemism designed to insulate the responsible officials from legal exposure. In a December 2007 ABC News interview, Kiriakou became the first U.S. official to publicly confirm that the CIA had waterboarded detainees and the first to characterize the technique as torture; he was subsequently prosecuted by the Obama administration under the [Espionage Act](/wiki/espionage-act) for an unrelated confirmation of a covert officer's name.

## Origin and timeline

Kiriakou traces the program's origin to a cocktail party approximately one month after September 11, 2001, at which someone with proximity to the leadership of the CIA's [Counterterrorism Center](/wiki/cia) approached Director [George Tenet](/wiki/george-tenet) and pitched a set of interrogation techniques. That person described two psychologists, [Mitchell and Jessen](/wiki/mitchell-and-jessen), who had worked on the Air Force's SERE (survival, evasion, resistance, and escape) training program and had reverse-engineered its techniques for use against detainees. For $108 million, they would contract with the CIA and implement what they proposed to call "enhanced interrogation techniques."<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="11:30" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="12:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="12:30" /><Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="55:34" /><Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="56:05" />

The CIA signed Mitchell and Jessen's contract in January 2002.<Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="55:34" /><Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="56:05" /> [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah) was captured in Pakistan in March 2002 and became the program's first subject; EIT was first applied to him on August 2, 2002.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="02:34" /><Cite s="2023-11-12-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-1" t="1:01" /> Kiriakou himself was approached and asked whether he would be certified in EIT during the first week of May 2002; he refused. His December 10, 2007 ABC News interview marked the first U.S. official public confirmation of CIA waterboarding.<Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="55:34" /><Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="56:05" />

While [John Brennan](/wiki/john-brennan) — who later became Director of the CIA — has publicly opposed torture, Kiriakou identifies him in private as the senior architect of the program: *"He's a guy who runs out and says, 'Yeah, we shouldn't torture people.' When he wrote the fucking torture — he was the godfather of the torture program."*<Cite s="2026-01-16-julian-dorey-french-intelligence" t="27:34" /> Brennan was part of the leadership structure that oversaw the program during the Bush administration.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="20:30" />

## The certification offer and Kiriakou's refusal

Kiriakou has given two closely related accounts of the offer he received to be certified in EIT. In one, a friend from the Counterterrorism Center approached him informally in the CIA cafeteria at agency headquarters in May 2002: *"Hey, I'm so glad I ran into you — I meant to ask, do you want to be certified in the use of enhanced interrogation techniques?"* Kiriakou had not previously heard the term. The friend listed ten techniques — waterboarding, sleep deprivation, a *"smack in the face,"* and others — and stated that *"the President approved it and the Justice Department approved it."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="00:31" /><Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="01:02" /> In another telling, he was approached while standing in the CIA cafeteria sandwich line by a senior officer who casually asked the same question; on hearing the description of the techniques, Kiriakou replied, *"Buddy, that sounds like a torture program."*<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="18:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="18:30" />

Kiriakou asked for time to think, went up to the agency's seventh floor (its executive level), and consulted a very senior CIA officer he had worked for a decade earlier in the Middle East. The officer's advice:

> First of all, let's call a spade a spade — this is a torture program. And you know how these guys are: somebody's going to go overboard, and they're going to kill a prisoner. And when that happens, there's going to be a congressional investigation and then a Justice Department investigation, and somebody's going to go to prison. You want to go to prison?<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="01:32" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="19:00" />

Kiriakou returned downstairs and declined certification.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="02:03" /> In total, fourteen CIA officers were offered EIT certification; two initially declined and one of those two subsequently changed his mind. Kiriakou was the only one who refused in the end. His reflection: *"The crazy thing is I knew these guys. I was friends with these guys. Our wives were friends. Our kids played together. I had no idea that they had the ability to become monsters, murderers."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="02:03" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="19:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="19:30" /> Kiriakou has also given an account, framed around this same 14/13 split, in which he separately sought counsel from a respected senior CIA officer who agreed the proposed methods might cross dangerous moral and legal lines, after which he declined the training.<Cite s="2026-03-09-dead-drop-s1e18-cross-currents" t="31:39" /> One of the 13 colleagues who agreed to the training came to him a day or two before it began and told him, when Kiriakou called it a mistake, that Justice Department attorneys had assured them it was not torture — to which Kiriakou replied only that they would have to agree to disagree.<Cite s="2026-04-20-dead-drop-s1e24-moment-of-truth" t="06:22" />

## Approved versus unauthorized techniques

A central distinction in the program, and the basis of Kiriakou's recurring argument for selective prosecution, is between techniques authorized in writing by the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel and acts administered by individual CIA officers on their own initiative.

**Among the approved techniques:** waterboarding; [sleep deprivation](/wiki/sleep-deprivation) (authorized up to 12 days continuous); [walling](/wiki/walling) in its formally designed variant (plywood with give, a rolled towel around the neck); and other items on the standard ten-technique list, including grabbing a shirt, a *"belly slap,"* and a face slap — which Kiriakou says were mostly *"not something I would call torture."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="01:02" /><Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="1:23:28" />

**Among the unauthorized techniques applied in practice** — which Kiriakou distinguishes sharply from the approved list and says were used against most if not all of the CIA's high-value prisoners without ever having been authorized by the Justice Department or the President:<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:49:11" /><Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:49:43" />

- **Forced rectal feeding with pureed [hummus](/wiki/hummus)** — *"you can't force hummus upside someone's rectum."*<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:49:43" />
- **Russian roulette with prisoners.**<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:49:43" />
- **The Abu Zubaydah cockroach coffin** — exploiting Abu Zubaydah's *"irrational fear of insects,"* interrogators sealed him in a coffin with a box of cockroaches and nothing but a diaper, and listened to him scream, for ten days.<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:50:14" /><Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="1:25:32" /><Cite s="2025-10-02-truth-hurts-epstein-mossad-blackmail-playbook" t="1:26:05" />
- **[Sleep deprivation](/wiki/sleep-deprivation) beyond the authorized window** — authorized for up to twelve days, even though, per the American Psychological Association's own research, *"people begin to go insane at seven days with no sleep, they begin to die of organ failure with nine days."* People died of it.<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:50:46" />
- **The [cold cell](/wiki/cold-cell)** — never authorized as an EIT technique, and fatal to two prisoners.<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:51:16" />
- **[Walling](/wiki/walling) without the designed safeguards** — supposed to use a rolled towel around the neck against a fiberboard wall; administered in practice with no towel, against a concrete wall. [Muhammad Atar](/wiki/muhammad-atar) is now permanently brain-damaged and unable to participate in his own defense as a result.<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:51:47" /><Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:52:18" />
- **Sexual assault using broomsticks.**<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="04:09" /><Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="05:13" /><Cite s="2015-02-09-democracy-now-torture-report" t="00:13" />

In late August 2023 the Department of Defense conceded that [Ramzi bin al-Shibh](/wiki/ramzi-bin-al-shibh) — one of the 9/11 co-conspirators — cannot be tried, because the Pentagon's own psychiatrist has determined him clinically insane as a direct result of sleep deprivation.<Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:52:18" /><Cite s="2023-11-19-dalton-fischer-kiriakou-part-2" t="1:52:48" />

### Rectal feeding and the exploitation of religious shame

Kiriakou has separately described rectal feeding as an authorized technique administered with a specifically cultural rather than nutritional purpose: tubes were inserted and liquid food — he specifies hummus — was pumped in, exploiting the particular shame attached in Islam to nakedness and bodily humiliation, especially before women.<Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:08:30" /><Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:09:00" /> He describes the deliberate use of female interrogators as a related humiliation mechanism: prisoners were stripped naked in the presence of women specifically because of those religious beliefs, combining nudity, cold-cell temperatures, and female presence to break religious dignity alongside physical endurance.<Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:08:00" /><Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:08:30" />

Kiriakou describes [sensory deprivation](/wiki/waterboarding) — an isolation tank with total silence, total darkness, and surrounding water, used for periods of up to three weeks — as, in his assessment, worse than waterboarding: *"You're complete and total. Like you're dead."*<Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:07:00" /><Cite s="2026-06-05-theo-von-podcast-661" t="1:07:30" />

## The psychology and justification of the program

The theory underpinning many of the techniques, as conceived by Mitchell and Jessen, is *"learned helplessness"* — a form of Pavlovian conditioning in which the prisoner comes to associate a sensory cue, such as the rolled towel placed around the neck before walling, with imminent impact, eventually crumpling on presentation of the cue alone.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="06:48" />

The program's standard public justification is the [ticking time bomb scenario](/wiki/ticking-time-bomb). Kiriakou rejects it on three grounds: it is illegal; there is no real-world equivalent of a ticking time bomb; and torture produces only what the prisoner believes the interrogator wants to hear, requiring months of analyst review to separate signal from noise — by which time any hypothetical bomb would already have gone off.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="07:52" /><Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="08:23" /> He has described the broader practical problem with torture-derived intelligence in the same terms: it must be verified, consuming months of analyst time and generating false leads that let adversaries continue planning. Subjects under torture provide partial truths — *"a kernel of truth"* — sufficient to stop the pain in the moment but designed to waste the interrogator's time.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="47:59" />

Kiriakou attributes the program's existence less to a genuine belief in its effectiveness than to two other forces. The first is a CIA personnel-selection failure: *"You put sociopaths in positions of authority where they come to believe that they're above the law, and that because they're the good guys they can and should do anything that they want to do."* He extends this characterization explicitly to [Jose Rodriguez](/wiki/jose-rodriguez) and to Mitchell and Jessen, all of whom he believes genuinely think they are the good guys.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="11:33" /><Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="12:04" /> The second is revenge: CIA leaders who had spent decades building careers felt their legacies would be defined by the failure to prevent the September 11 attacks, and *"had to do literally anything to stop them. And even if it's not to stop them, it's to pay them back for what they did."* Kiriakou recalls a CIA supervisor whispering *"Kill them all"* to him as he departed for Pakistan in the months after 9/11 — cited by Kiriakou as evidence that revenge, not intelligence-gathering, was the true motivator.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="48:29" /><Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="49:34" /> *"It's terrible. But we can't underestimate the role of revenge."*<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="47:59" />

Kiriakou has also noted the direct contradiction between the program and [CIA training doctrine](/wiki/cia-training-doctrine): operational training explicitly teaches that coercion and threats are never to be used in recruiting or handling sources, not only because they are unethical but because they are ineffective and can provoke violent retaliation. Recruits were trained instead that asset development works through establishing genuine friendship and rapport.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="40:40" />

A colleague at Liberty University, where Kiriakou taught for several years, sent him a final exam built around the ticking-time-bomb scenario. Its first three questions escalated progressively — would the student use enhanced interrogation on a suspect, on the suspect's wife, on the suspect's children in front of the parents — each requiring the student to explain the answer. The fourth and final question asked: *"You've died and you're standing before the judgment seat of Christ and he tells you to explain your actions. What do you tell him?"* Kiriakou described the question as leaving him speechless and said he still thinks about it years later.<Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="55:30" /><Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="56:00" />

## Institutional resistance: the FBI

The FBI was so deeply at odds with the CIA over the program that its personnel would leave the country entirely rather than be present. Every time Mitchell and Jessen took charge of an interrogation session at a CIA black site, every FBI representative in the country would leave: *"The FBI didn't even want to be in the same country where this torture was taking place."*<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="09:28" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="24:30" />

## The fabricated Zubaydah result

Kiriakou has described as an error his statement in the December 2007 Brian Ross interview that Abu Zubaydah had been waterboarded once and that the waterboarding produced intelligence. He now says this was incorrect, and that he said it because Mitchell and Jessen had reported to the CIA that they waterboarded Zubaydah once and he produced intelligence as a result.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="36:30" />

The reality, not revealed until 2005, when the CIA inspector general investigated: FBI interrogator [Ali Soufan](/wiki/ali-soufan) had been obtaining actionable intelligence from Zubaydah daily through rapport-based interrogation, cabling his findings back to the FBI. Those cables never reached the CIA because the FBI's and CIA's computer systems were incompatible with each other. On August 2, 2002, Director Tenet asked President Bush to transfer primacy over the Zubaydah interrogation from the FBI to the CIA. That same day, the CIA began torturing Zubaydah; he immediately went silent and stopped providing intelligence.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="35:30" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="36:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="36:30" />

Mitchell and Jessen then accessed the FBI's system, pulled Soufan's cables, retyped them into the CIA's system, and reported: *"We waterboarded him once and look what he told us."* The CIA inspector general confirmed in 2005 that the intelligence had been fabricated as their own product; the finding was not declassified until 2009.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="37:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="37:30" />

## Deaths in custody and body disposal

When prisoners died during EIT sessions — fatalities occurring particularly from hypothermia in the cold cell and from organ failure under prolonged sleep deprivation — the standard disposal procedure at the secret site was on-site burial: *"When people would die, they would just dig a hole next to the interrogation building, put him in the hole, cover it up, and then bring the next guy in."* In at least one documented case, disposal was delayed pending direction from headquarters; the instruction relayed was *"put him on ice until we can figure out what to do,"* which personnel on the ground interpreted literally, placing the body in a bathtub filled with ice until it began to decompose.<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="20:55" />

Kiriakou, from his post as Executive Assistant to the CIA's Deputy Director for Operations, disclosed receiving a cable reporting that a prisoner held in a cold cell *"didn't make it through the night."* The facility did not know what to do with the body: it could not be buried locally, and it could not be returned to the man's family. Personnel packed the body in ice and cremated it; the man's existence was effectively erased.<Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="1:07:00" /><Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="1:07:30" />

A related internal consequence Kiriakou documented from the same post: a CIA secretary fainted during an Abu Zubaydah interrogation session and subsequently *"curtailed her assignment,"* sending an unprompted cable to headquarters announcing her return — *"a career-ending decision to curtail an assignment."* Kiriakou describes the episode as one of several similar curtailments demonstrating that he was not alone in his moral assessment of the program, and yet *"certainly somebody's going to come out and say something. And nobody did."*<Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="17:45" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="18:16" />

Kiriakou described sleep deprivation as authorized for up to twelve days under the program, citing the American Psychological Association's finding that people begin to lose their minds at day seven without sleep and enter organ failure at day nine. He noted that Defense Secretary Don Rumsfeld had a stand-up desk and claimed to work twenty-four hours without sitting, and did not believe prolonged sleep deprivation was unusual. Kiriakou's assessment: keeping someone awake for twelve days is torture.<Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="1:08:00" /><Cite s="2026-04-16-hang-out-with-sean-hannity-lawfare-template" t="1:08:30" />

## Authorization, legal architecture, and accountability

The program was not a rogue operation. According to Kiriakou, torture was official U.S. policy, personally authorized by President George W. Bush, who signed off on the program and its specific techniques. Kiriakou describes this as a distinction that matters: the people who designed and conducted the program were following orders from the highest level of the executive branch, not acting outside sanctioned channels.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="20:00" />

Kiriakou describes the legal architecture around the program as a deliberate reinterpretation of existing law. The [Federal Torture Act](/wiki/federal-torture-act) had been on the books since 1946, with documented enforcement history including the execution of Japanese soldiers after World War II. In 2002, Justice Department opinions effectively suspended that history: *"But then in 2002, like magic, it's all legal."*<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="22:00" /> Even so, Kiriakou draws a hard line around what the Justice Department did and did not sanction: it never authorized smashing prisoners' heads against a wall until they suffered brain damage, freezing them to death, or keeping them awake until their organs failed and then burying them in a ditch beside the interrogation building — nobody at the CIA, he says, ever had permission to do that.<Cite s="2026-04-20-dead-drop-s1e24-moment-of-truth" t="09:33" /> He has separately stressed that the CIA has never historically been in the business of interrogation and had no training or institutional mandate for it, with narrow exceptions during the Vietnam War and, after September 11, the enhanced interrogation program itself.<Cite s="2026-04-13-dead-drop-s1e23-good-company-bad-company" t="07:43" />

Nothing obtained under EIT from Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, Abu Zubaydah, Ramzi bin al-Shibh, or Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri is admissible at trial, *"because the CIA tortured it out of him."* This has left the U.S. government with the choice of holding the detainees indefinitely without charge or releasing them.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="08:54" />

White House counsel Harriet Miers explicitly told Jose Rodriguez, then CIA Deputy Director for Operations, and [Gina Haspel](/wiki/gina-haspel), then head of counterterrorism, not to destroy the videotapes of the torture sessions. As soon as Rodriguez and Haspel returned to CIA headquarters, they put all the tapes through an industrial grinder and destroyed them.<Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="38:00" /><Cite s="2025-07-09-pbd-podcast-epstein-ghislaine-maxwell-israel" t="38:30" />

According to Kiriakou, the program ran for approximately four years before being shut down in 2006. The internal determination was that the information obtained under torture *"wasn't amounting to anything."* Kiriakou attributes the shutdown decision to CIA Director Porter Goss.<Cite s="2025-04-23-dr-phil-podcast-secrets-lies-price-of-truth-part2" t="8:21" />

As of the most recent source in KiriPedia's corpus, no CIA officer has been criminally prosecuted in connection with any technique applied under the program, authorized or unauthorized: *"Here we are twenty-two years after 9/11 — literally no one has been prosecuted for that crime. No one. Nor will anyone be prosecuted, because the CIA ensured that no prosecutions could be carried out."* Mitchell and Jessen's 2001–2002 contract with the CIA — to develop the program from their reverse-engineering of U.S. military SERE training — was worth $108 million: *"We paid those guys $108 million to say, 'Oh, we think you should torture people. Here are the torture techniques. Just let us know when you want us to start.'"* The only person to have served federal prison time in connection with the program is Kiriakou himself, for an unrelated Espionage Act prosecution.<Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="09:28" /><Cite s="2023-11-26-dalton-fischer-enhanced-interrogation" t="10:30" /><Cite s="2025-10-10-joe-rogan-experience-2392" t="28:40" />

The CIA's program is also documented, in brief, in the [2014 whistleblower feature documentary](/wiki/john-kiriakou) *Silenced*: the agency initiated a program referred to as "enhanced interrogation techniques," which Kiriakou has publicly referred to as torture.<Cite s="2025-10-14-jspione-silenced-2014-the-war-on-whistleblowers-feature-documentary-" t="29:04" /><Cite s="2025-10-14-jspione-silenced-2014-the-war-on-whistleblowers-feature-documentary-" t="29:34" />
